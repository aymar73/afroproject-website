Cointegration: The Marriage License of Currencies

October 2026 · 7 min read

In 1999, eleven European countries adopted the euro. They did so for political reasons: the memory of two world wars, the desire for European unity, the ambition of a continent speaking with one economic voice. What they did not do — what they could not do with the statistical tools of the time — was prove that their economies were actually compatible.

Ten years later, the euro nearly collapsed.

The Greek Tragedy

When the 2008 global financial crisis hit, Germany recovered within two years. Greece entered a depression that lasted a decade. Unemployment in Greece peaked at 27%. The country required three international bailouts. Its economy shrank by 25%.

Why? Because Greece and Germany were never economically synchronized. Germany's economy is driven by manufacturing exports. Greece's economy is driven by tourism and shipping. When a global shock hits, these two economies respond in opposite ways.

In a single currency zone, there is only one monetary policy. The European Central Bank set interest rates that suited Germany — the largest economy — but were disastrous for Greece. Greece could not devalue its currency (it no longer had one). It could not set its own interest rates (the ECB did that). It was trapped.

The euro was a political marriage between two economies that were not economically compatible. The divorce — when it nearly came — would have destroyed the continent.

What Is Cointegration?

Cointegration is a statistical property that measures whether two or more economies move together in the long run. If two economies are cointegrated, they share a common trend. When one drifts away from the other, forces pull them back together.

Think of it this way: two boats tied together by a long rope. Each boat can drift independently in the short term — one might be pushed by a wave, the other by a gust of wind. But the rope ensures they never drift too far apart. Over time, they move together.

The "rope" is the cointegrating relationship. The statistical test — developed by Søren Johansen in 1991 — measures how strong that rope is.

The Mathematical Foundation

If two time series, \( X_t \) and \( Y_t \), are both integrated of order 1 (meaning they are non-stationary, but their first differences are stationary), they are cointegrated if there exists a linear combination: 

Z_t = X_t - β·Y_t

that is stationary. The parameter \( \beta \) is the cointegrating vector — it describes the long-run equilibrium relationship between the two series.

If \( Z_t \) is stationary, it means that whenever \( X_t \) and \( Y_t \) drift apart, the "error" \( Z_t \) tends to return to zero. This is the statistical equivalent of the rope.

The Johansen Test

For more than two variables, the Johansen Trace Test extends this framework. It tests the null hypothesis that there are \( r \) cointegrating relationships against the alternative that there are more.

The test statistic is: 

LR_trace(r) = -T · Σ ln(1 - λ_i)

where \( T \) is the sample size and \( λ_i \) are the eigenvalues from the estimation. If the trace statistic exceeds the critical value at the 5% level, we reject the null and conclude that cointegration exists.

Why This Matters for Africa

The Euro crisis was not inevitable. It was the result of a design flaw: political leaders created a monetary union without first proving that the member economies were compatible.

Africa can do better. We have the data. We have the statistical tools. We have the computational power. There is no excuse for repeating Europe's mistake.

In the Afro Project, cointegration serves as the gatekeeper for monetary union. A country cannot join a currency zone unless it is statistically proven to be cointegrated with the existing members. This is not a political decision. It is a mathematical one.

Cointegration is the marriage license of currencies. Without it, monetary union is not integration — it is a handcuff.

The Results

We ran the Johansen Trace Test on 20 years of monthly data from 54 African countries. The results were unambiguous:

The finding is clear: within each of the three zones, cointegration is nearly universal. Across zones, it is almost non-existent. This is why a single African currency would fail — and why a three-zone architecture is the only statistically viable path.
