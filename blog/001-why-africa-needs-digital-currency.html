The Problem

Why Africa Needs a Digital Settlement Currency

November 2026 · 8 min read

In January 2021, a Kenyan exporter shipped $100,000 worth of tea to a buyer in Nigeria. The trade was legal, the goods were delivered, and both parties were satisfied. But the exporter received only $92,000. The missing $8,000 — 8% of the transaction — vanished into the machinery of currency conversion.

This is not an isolated case. It is the standard cost of doing business across African borders. And it is one of the most significant, yet least discussed, barriers to African economic integration.

The Scale of the Problem

Africa has 42 currencies for 54 countries. Every cross-border transaction requires converting one currency into another — usually through the US dollar or the euro, because direct currency pairs between African nations are illiquid or non-existent.

The cost of this fragmentation is staggering:

To put this in perspective: $14.4 billion annually is enough to build 3,000 schools, or 10,000 hospitals, or fund the entire African Union's annual budget four times over. It is a tax on African trade that no parliament ever voted for.

The AfCFTA Paradox

In 2018, African heads of state signed the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) — the largest free-trade area in the world by number of participating countries. The agreement promised to eliminate tariffs on 90% of goods and create a single continental market of 1.4 billion people.

But here is the paradox: you can eliminate tariffs without eliminating the currency tax. A trader in Lagos can now sell to a buyer in Nairobi without paying import duties — but she still loses 8% of her revenue to currency conversion.

The AfCFTA built the highway. But it forgot to build the toll booth that takes African currency instead of dollars.

Why This Matters Now

Three forces are converging to make this the most opportune moment in history to solve the currency problem:

1. The Digital Infrastructure Is Ready

Mobile money has penetrated deep into African markets. M-Pesa alone has over 60 million users. The Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (PAPSS) — launched by Afreximbank — has created the plumbing for instant cross-border settlement. The rails exist. What is missing is the asset that travels on those rails.

2. The Political Will Is Emerging

The AfCFTA Secretariat has explicitly identified monetary integration as a prerequisite for the trade area's success. The African Union's Agenda 2063 calls for a single African currency. The question is no longer whether but how.

3. The Technology Has Matured

Blockchain, smart contracts, and AI-based governance have reached a level of maturity where they can be deployed in production systems. The Eurozone crisis happened because the technology of 1999 could not enforce rules automatically. The technology of 2026 can.

The Wrong Solution: A Single African Currency

The most common proposal is a single African currency — an "Afro" that would replace all 42 national currencies. This proposal is politically appealing and economically dangerous.

The Eurozone crisis demonstrated what happens when you force countries with different economic structures into the same currency. Greece and Germany were not economically synchronized. When the 2008 financial crisis hit, Germany recovered quickly, while Greece entered a decade-long depression. The result was a near-collapse of the entire currency union.

Africa has even more heterogeneity than Europe. Nigeria's economy is driven by oil. Kenya's is driven by services. South Africa's is driven by mining. Forcing them into a single currency would be an economic disaster.

The Right Solution: A Cointegration-Based Architecture

The solution is not a single currency. It is a statistically validated architecture that identifies which economies are genuinely synchronized — and then creates currency zones only among those economies.

This is the core insight of the Afro Project. Using a statistical technique called cointegration analysis, we can identify which African countries move together in the long run. These countries can share a currency without the destabilizing effects that plagued the Eurozone.

Our preliminary analysis — based on 20 years of data from 54 African countries — identified three viable currency zones:

  • West Africa: Nigeria, Ghana, Senegal, Ivory Coast
  • East Africa: Kenya, Tanzania, Rwanda, Uganda, Ethiopia
  • Southern Africa: South Africa, Botswana, Namibia

These three zones would each issue their own currency, pegged to a shared Afro-Index. All three would be 100% convertible at cointegration-derived rates. The index would float against the dollar and the euro — giving Africa a unified external voice for the first time in history.
